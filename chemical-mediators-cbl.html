Before you begin

This session follows three patients seen at a Klang Valley hospital. Each one shows a different side of the same story: injured or irritated tissue releases chemical signals, and those signals produce redness, swelling, pain, pus, fever and sometimes shock. Work through the cases in order. Answer each checkpoint before you read on, because the next part of the case often gives the answer away.

Learning outcomes

By the end of this session you should be able to:

  1. Define a chemical mediator of inflammation and classify mediators by origin (cell-derived or plasma-derived) and by whether they are preformed or newly made.
  2. Describe the source and main actions of vasoactive amines, arachidonic acid metabolites, PAF, cytokines and chemokines, complement, the kinin and clotting systems, nitric oxide and reactive oxygen species.
  3. Link each cardinal sign of inflammation, and the systemic acute-phase response, to the mediators that cause it.
  4. Explain how common drugs (NSAIDs, aspirin, antihistamines, leukotriene antagonists, corticosteroids, adrenaline) act on these mediators, and why some of them cause problems.

Basics: what is a chemical mediator?

Inflammation brings leukocytes and plasma proteins out of the blood into the tissue, where they kill microbes and clear debris. None of this happens by accident. It is ordered by chemical mediators: substances that start, amplify and regulate the inflammatory reaction.

Core concept
  • Two origins. Cell-derived mediators come from mast cells, platelets, neutrophils, macrophages and endothelium. Plasma-derived mediators (complement, kinins, clotting factors) circulate as inactive precursors, mostly made in the liver, and must be cleaved to work.
  • Preformed or newly made. Histamine sits ready in mast cell granules and is released in seconds. Prostaglandins, leukotrienes and cytokines are synthesised only after the cell is stimulated, so they arrive later.
  • How they act. Most bind specific receptors on target cells. A few act directly as enzymes (lysosomal proteases), and some cause oxidative damage (oxygen free radicals).
  • Cascades. One mediator can trigger release of another. C5a, for example, makes mast cells release histamine.
  • Short-lived. Once released they decay quickly, are broken down by enzymes (histaminase, kininase) or are blocked by inhibitors. This check-and-balance matters because nearly every mediator can also damage the host.

CASE 1about 50 min

A cut on the shin

Encik Faizal, 34, construction worker. On Monday morning a piece of rusty steel bar scrapes his left shin. It bleeds a little. He rinses it at the site tap and keeps working.

Within a few minutes the skin around the cut turns pink and a small raised weal forms.

Stage 1 The first few minutes: redness and swelling

The redness is arteriolar dilation, which brings more blood (and warmth) to the area. The swelling is fluid leaking from post-capillary venules into the tissue. Something must be acting on the vessels almost instantly. That rules out anything the cell has to make from scratch.

Core content: vasoactive amines

Histamine

  • Stored preformed in mast cells (the richest source, especially in connective tissue next to vessels), basophils and platelets.
  • Released by physical injury, cold or heat, IgE-mediated allergen binding, the anaphylatoxins C3a and C5a, substance P and some cytokines (IL-1, IL-8).
  • Acts on H1 receptors: dilates arterioles and increases venular permeability by making endothelial cells contract, which opens gaps between them. Also contracts bronchial smooth muscle.
  • Called the main mediator of the immediate transient phase of increased permeability. It is broken down within minutes by histaminase.

Serotonin (5-hydroxytryptamine)

  • In humans, stored in platelets and in neuroendocrine (enterochromaffin) cells of the gut. Released when platelets aggregate.
  • Its main vascular effect is vasoconstriction. Its role in human inflammation is minor compared with histamine. (In rodents it is also in mast cells, which is where older notes got this idea.)
Core content: nitric oxide (NO)
  • A soluble gas, first described as "endothelium-derived relaxing factor". Made from L-arginine and oxygen by nitric oxide synthase (NOS).
  • Three forms: eNOS (endothelium) and nNOS (neurons) are always present at low level; iNOS is induced in macrophages and other cells by cytokines such as IFN-γ and TNF and by microbial products.
  • Actions: relaxes vascular smooth muscle (vasodilation), reduces platelet adhesion and aggregation, damps leukocyte recruitment, and in macrophages it kills microbes (combined with superoxide it forms peroxynitrite).
  • Too much iNOS-derived NO in sepsis causes widespread vasodilation and refractory hypotension.

Stage 2 That evening: throbbing pain

By evening the cut is hot, swollen and throbbing. The redness has spread about 3 cm around it. Pressing the area hurts. Bacteria from the steel and his skin (most likely Streptococcus pyogenes or Staphylococcus aureus) are multiplying in the dermis.

Two new things are happening. Bacteria are activating the plasma protein systems, and cells in the tissue have had time to make prostaglandins. Both contribute to pain.

Core content: complement

A set of more than 20 plasma proteins, made in the liver, that circulate inactive. Activation is a cascade of proteolytic cleavages. The key step in every route is cleavage of C3.

  • Classical pathway: C1 binds antibody (IgG or IgM) attached to an antigen.
  • Alternative pathway: triggered directly by microbial surfaces, endotoxin (LPS), complex polysaccharides. No antibody needed.
  • Lectin pathway: mannose-binding lectin in plasma binds sugars on microbial surfaces.

Core content: the kinin and clotting systems

Injured tissue exposes collagen and basement membrane. Factor XII (Hageman factor) touches these surfaces and becomes XIIa. That single event starts four systems at once.

  • Kinin system: XIIa converts prekallikrein to kallikrein. Kallikrein cleaves high-molecular-weight kininogen (HMWK) to release bradykinin. Kallikrein also activates more factor XII (a positive loop) and can cleave C5 to C5a.
  • Bradykinin increases vascular permeability, dilates arterioles, contracts bronchial smooth muscle and causes pain when injected into skin. It is inactivated fast by kininases, one of which is angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE).
  • Clotting system: XIIa drives the intrinsic pathway to thrombin. Thrombin turns fibrinogen into fibrin and also acts on protease-activated receptors (PARs) on platelets, endothelium and leukocytes, increasing inflammation. Fibrinopeptides increase permeability.
  • Fibrinolytic system: plasmin breaks down fibrin (fibrin split products raise permeability) and can cleave C3.
Clinical corner

This is why ACE inhibitors (enalapril, perindopril) can cause a dry cough and, rarely, sudden swelling of the lips and tongue. Bradykinin is not broken down and accumulates. Antihistamines don't help, because histamine isn't the culprit.

Stage 3 Day 2: pus

Tuesday afternoon, Faizal comes to the emergency department. The wound is oozing thick yellow pus. A Gram stain of the pus shows sheets of neutrophils and Gram-positive cocci in chains.

Pus is mostly neutrophils, live and dead. They had to leave the vessels and travel up a chemical gradient to reach the bacteria. Then, while killing bacteria, they spilled toxic products into the surrounding tissue.

Core content: chemokines and other chemoattractants

Neutrophils follow several chemical trails: bacterial N-formyl-methionyl peptides, C5a, leukotriene B4 and chemokines.

Chemokines are small proteins (8 to 10 kD) that drive both chemokinesis and directed chemotaxis. They also activate leukocyte integrins so the cells stick firmly to endothelium. They fall into four classes by the spacing of their first cysteine residues:

Core content: reactive oxygen species (ROS) and lysosomal enzymes
  • Phagocytosis switches on NADPH oxidase (phagocyte oxidase), the "respiratory burst". Oxygen becomes superoxide, then H2O2. Myeloperoxidase turns H2O2 and chloride into hypochlorous acid (HOCl, the bleach that kills bacteria).
  • Some ROS and lysosomal enzymes (elastase, collagenase) leak out of the cell. Outside, they damage endothelium (more leak), injure nearby cells and red cells, and inactivate antiproteases such as α1-antitrypsin, which leaves elastase free to digest matrix.
  • The body defends itself with antioxidants: superoxide dismutase, catalase, glutathione peroxidase, and the plasma proteins ceruloplasmin and transferrin.

Stage 4 Day 2: he is unwell all over

Faizal says he had chills last night and couldn't eat. He feels tired and achy.

Temp 38.9 °CHR 108/minBP 122/76WBC 15.8 × 10⁹/L (neutrophils 84%, some band forms)CRP 92 mg/L

A local problem has become a systemic one. Something made in the leg is now acting on his brain, liver and bone marrow.

Core content: the key cytokines, TNF, IL-1 and IL-6

Cytokines are small proteins made mainly by activated macrophages, dendritic cells and lymphocytes, but also by mast cells, endothelium and epithelium. In acute inflammation the big three are TNF, IL-1 and IL-6. Bacterial products such as LPS, and the inflammasome (which activates IL-1β), push macrophages to make them.

Local effects

  • Activate endothelium: more selectins and integrin ligands, so leukocytes stick
  • More chemokine and PGI2 production
  • Endothelium becomes procoagulant
  • Prime and activate neutrophils and macrophages

Systemic effects (acute-phase response)

  • Fever: IL-1 and TNF raise COX-2 and PGE2 in the hypothalamus, resetting the thermostat
  • Acute-phase proteins: IL-6 (helped by IL-1, TNF) drives the liver to make CRP, fibrinogen, serum amyloid A
  • Leukocytosis: release of neutrophils from the marrow, with CSFs driving production (hence the band forms)
  • Sleepiness, loss of appetite; TNF in chronic disease causes cachexia
  • In sepsis, huge amounts of TNF cause shock (low BP, DIC)

IL-17, made by Th17 cells, also recruits neutrophils, and anti-IL-17 drugs are used in psoriasis.

No. Paracetamol lowers the hypothalamic set point by reducing PGE2 there. It doesn't touch the bacteria or the IL-1/TNF that the macrophages keep making. The fever has been masked, not treated. Faizal still needs antibiotics (flucloxacillin or similar for cellulitis), elevation of the leg and a check that no abscess needs draining.

Fever itself has some benefit (slower bacterial growth, better leukocyte function), but at 38.9 °C with rigors comfort matters too.

CASE 2about 25 min

Satay at the wedding

Aina, 21, a university student, is at a cousin's wedding reception. She knows she is allergic to peanuts (her lips once tingled after a cookie) but forgets to ask about the satay sauce.

Five minutes after eating: itchy palms, then raised red weals across her chest and arms. Her lips swell. At 10 minutes her voice is hoarse and she starts to wheeze. A relative drives her to the nearest emergency department.

BP 80/45HR 128/minRR 28/minSpO₂ 90% on airWidespread urticaria, lip and tongue swelling, wheeze

She gets IM adrenaline 0.5 mg into the thigh, oxygen, IV fluids, an antihistamine (chlorphenamine) and IV hydrocortisone. She improves within 15 minutes. The doctor still keeps her overnight for observation.

Core content: arachidonic acid (AA) metabolites

When a cell is stimulated, phospholipase A2 cuts arachidonic acid (a 20-carbon fatty acid) out of the membrane phospholipids. Two families of enzymes then turn AA into short-range lipid messengers called eicosanoids.

  • COX-1 is present in most tissues all the time (stomach lining, platelets, kidney). COX-2 is induced at sites of inflammation by cytokines.
  • Cysteinyl leukotrienes (LTC4, LTD4, LTE4), once called "slow-reacting substance of anaphylaxis", come from mast cells. They are far more potent than histamine at contracting bronchi and making venules leak.
  • LTB4 from neutrophils and macrophages is a strong chemoattractant.
  • Lipoxins are made when neutrophils and platelets cooperate (transcellular synthesis). They inhibit neutrophil recruitment and help inflammation resolve.
Core content: platelet-activating factor (PAF)

A phospholipid mediator, also produced by phospholipase A2 action, made by mast cells, platelets, leukocytes and endothelium. At tiny concentrations it dilates vessels and makes venules leak (100 to 10,000 times more potent than histamine on a molar basis). It also causes bronchoconstriction, platelet aggregation, leukocyte adhesion, chemotaxis, degranulation and oxidative burst. PAF can produce nearly all the features of acute inflammation on its own, and blood PAF levels track the severity of anaphylaxis.

Adrenaline reverses the effects of every mediator at once, not just one. Its α1 action constricts arterioles and tightens leaky vessels (BP up, less airway swelling), β2 relaxes bronchi, and β2 effects on mast cells reduce further release. An antihistamine blocks only H1 receptors. It does nothing to leukotrienes, PAF or the hypotension, so it treats the itch and weals but cannot save her airway.

Observation: mast cells and recruited eosinophils keep making leukotrienes and cytokines for hours. A late-phase (biphasic) reaction can return 4 to 12 hours later without any new exposure. Hydrocortisone is given to blunt this later wave, since steroids act on gene transcription and take hours to work. She should go home with an adrenaline auto-injector and a referral to an allergy clinic.

CASE 3about 20 min

The painkiller that caused wheeze

Puan Rajeswari, 46, a schoolteacher, has had asthma since her thirties and recurrent nasal polyps. Her knee has been aching after a fall, so her neighbour gives her two ibuprofen tablets.

About 45 minutes later her nose runs, her face flushes and she becomes very breathless with a loud wheeze. Her salbutamol inhaler helps only a little. At the clinic her peak flow is 40% of her best. There is no urticaria and no drop in blood pressure.

Core content: NSAID-exacerbated respiratory disease

Puan Rajeswari has the classic triad: asthma, nasal polyps and a reaction to aspirin or other COX-1 inhibitors. It is not an IgE allergy. In these patients, blocking COX does two things. Less PGE2 is made, and PGE2 normally holds mast cells back. And arachidonic acid that can't go down the COX route is shunted into the 5-lipoxygenase pathway, producing a burst of cysteinyl leukotrienes. Leukotriene receptor antagonists (montelukast) help these patients. Paracetamol at ordinary doses, and selective COX-2 inhibitors, are usually tolerated.

A week later her husband, Encik Kumar, 58, has an angioplasty after a heart attack. He is sent home on aspirin 75 mg daily "for life". He asks why such a small dose, when his wife's painkillers came in 400 mg tablets.

How inflammation switches off

Mediators are short-lived by design. Neutrophils die within hours to a day. And as the reaction goes on, the signals themselves change: cells switch from making leukotrienes to lipoxins, and from omega-3 fatty acids make resolvins and protectins. Macrophages that have eaten dead neutrophils release IL-10 and TGF-β, both anti-inflammatory. Neural reflexes (the vagal "cholinergic anti-inflammatory pathway") reduce TNF output. If these brakes fail, acute inflammation drifts into chronic inflammation.

Sensory nerve endings release substance P and neurokinin A when tissue is injured. Substance P transmits pain signals, increases vascular permeability, makes mast cells degranulate and helps regulate blood pressure. Nerve fibres rich in substance P are common in the lung and gut. This "neurogenic inflammation" is part of why a scratch on the skin produces a spreading flare.
